# SKAD – research design

> How the Sociology of Knowledge Approach to Discourse links the purpose of a study, its research questions and
> its theory. Written for SKAD only; overlaps with other methods are kept on purpose (`methods/README.md`). Status:
> draft from secondary summaries – to be checked against Keller (2011) and the 2018 volume (`sources.md`).

## What SKAD is for

SKAD (Reiner Keller, *Wissenssoziologische Diskursanalyse*, 2005) studies how knowledge is produced, stabilised and
changed in discourses: how a phenomenon becomes a problem, who may speak about it, which meanings and orders of
things are offered, and what follows from them for people and practices. It joins the sociology of knowledge of
Berger and Luckmann (social construction of reality) with Foucault's questions about discourse and power/knowledge,
and it works with the interpretive tools of qualitative social research (hermeneutics, grounded-theory coding).

The purpose of a SKAD study is therefore never to find out what a phenomenon "really is", but how it is made
meaningful in discourse and with what effects.

## Purpose and goals

A study states:
- **why** this discourse matters (a public controversy, a new phenomenon, a change in how something is talked
  about);
- **what** the researcher wants to find out about the discourse (its structure, its actors, its effects, its
  change, its competition with other discourses);
- **for whom** (scholarly debate, practice, public);
- **the researcher's position** – SKAD is interpretive; the researcher's own knowledge of the field is part of the
  frame and is written down (reflexive memo).

## How research questions arise

Questions are about the discourse. Typical families (to be checked against Keller's lists):
- **Emergence and change:** when and how does the discourse appear, what changes in it?
- **Content structure:** which interpretive schemes, classifications, phenomenon structure and story line does it
  use?
- **Actors and positions:** who speaks, from which speaker positions, with what legitimacy; who is not heard?
- **Subject positions and practices:** what identities does the discourse offer its addressees; what practices
  does it recommend or rule out?
- **Relations:** how does it relate to competing discourses; in which arenas does it appear?
- **Effects (dispositif):** what infrastructure produces it and what does it do in the world?

The first questions are broad. They are refined while the corpus is built and the first passages are coded;
every change of a question is recorded with the memo or finding that caused it.

## Role of prior theory

Theory is a **heuristic frame**, not a source of hypotheses. The SKAD concepts (interpretive scheme,
classification, phenomenon structure, narrative structure, speaker position, subject position, dispositif) and
the theory base (Berger & Luckmann, Foucault) work as sensitising concepts: they tell where to look, the data tell
what is there. Other theories relevant to the field (here e.g. theories of technology and identity) may enter the
frame the same way and are listed in the topic.

## Reasoning

Hermeneutic and non-deductive. Fine analysis of passages (sequential interpretation, several readings side by
side), open coding and constant comparison as in grounded theory, then abstraction to the content structure.
Abductive moments: a passage that does not fit the frame is a reason to revise it.

## Unit of analysis and corpus

- A discourse is reconstructed from a **corpus** of documents, not from one text. One document (e.g. one book) is
  one discursive event; it can start the study, but conclusions about a discourse need comparison.
- The corpus is built by **theoretical sampling**: minimal contrast (similar documents, to saturate a scheme) and
  maximal contrast (different arenas, speakers, genres).
- **Context** belongs to the analysis: situational (occasion, author, medium), institutional and organisational
  (publisher, events, field), socio-historical. Context sources (reviews, interviews, announcements) are part of
  the corpus or its frame.

## Theory output

A reconstruction of the discourse(s): content structure, speaker and subject positions, practices, relations to
other discourses and effects. It is stated as **propositions** that answer the research questions, each with
evidence and counter-evidence segments and a memo on how it was reached. It is a middle-range account of this
discourse, open to revision when the corpus grows.

## Quality criteria

- Traceability: every interpretation leads to segments and to the step that produced it.
- Plausibility of readings: alternative readings are written down and the chosen one is argued.
- Saturation of schemes by minimal contrast; scope tested by maximal contrast.
- Reflexivity: the researcher's position and the frame are explicit and revisited.

## Working with the result

- New documents are checked against the reconstruction: they confirm, extend or contradict it.
- The reconstruction is compared with other studies of the same or neighbouring discourses.
- New questions follow, including questions for a quantitative strand (e.g. how widespread a scheme is in a larger
  corpus or in a survey).
